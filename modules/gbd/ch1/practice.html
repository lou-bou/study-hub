Graph basics (Notions de base)

↑ top

Lesson sections 1 to 3

1.1Warm-up
Read graph X.
  1. Give its order n and size m.
  2. Give the degree of every vertex, then Δ(X) and δ(X).
  3. Name the leaves and the isolated vertices.
  4. Check that the degrees add up to 2m.
  5. List the edges incident to e and the vertices adjacent to e. What are the two ends of the edge bd?
Show solution

(a) n = 10, m = 11.

(b) d(a) = d(b) = d(c) = d(d) = d(e) = 3, d(f) = d(g) = d(h) = 2, d(i) = 1, d(j) = 0. So Δ = 3 and δ = 0.

(c) Leaf: i (degree 1). Isolated vertex: j (degree 0).

(d) 5·3 + 3·2 + 1 + 0 = 22 = 2·11. Each edge is counted once from each end.

(e) Edges incident to e: de, ef, eg. Vertices adjacent to e: d, f, g. The edge bd has ends b and d.

1.2Warm-up
Compute:
  1. N(d) and N[d].
  2. N(S) and N[S] for the set S = {a, h}.
Show solution

(a) N(d) = {b, c, e} and N[d] = {b, c, d, e}.

(b) N(a) = {b, c, i} and N(h) = {f, g}, so N(S) = {b, c, f, g, i}. Then N[S] = N(S) ∪ S = {a, b, c, f, g, h, i}. Since a and h are not adjacent, neither is in N(S).

1.3Standard
Find every pair of true twins and every pair of false twins in X. (False twins: non-adjacent with the same open neighbourhood. True twins: adjacent with the same closed neighbourhood.) Hint: write N(v) for every vertex first.
Show solution

True twins: b and c, since N[b] = N[c] = {a, b, c, d}.

False twins: f and g. They are not adjacent and N(f) = N(g) = {e, h}.

Nothing else works. For example a and d are not twins because N(a) = {b, c, i} ≠ {b, c, e} = N(d). The isolated vertex j has N(j) = ∅, and no other vertex has an empty neighbourhood.

1.4Warm-up
Which of these describe a simple graph? Explain each answer.
  1. V = {1,2,3}, E = {12, 23, 31}.
  2. V = {1,2,3} with the edge 23 drawn twice, plus the edge 12.
  3. V = {1,2} with a loop at 1 and the edge 12.
  4. V = {1,2,3,4}, E = {12, 34}.
Show solution

(a) Simple: no loop, no repeated pair.

(b) Not simple: two edges join the same pair {2,3}.

(c) Not simple: a loop (boucle) at vertex 1.

(d) Simple. A simple graph does not have to be connected.

1.5Standard
Subgraphs of X.
  1. List the edges and the degrees of the induced subgraph X[{d, e, f, g, h}].
  2. How many edges does X − d have?
  3. Let U = {ab, bc, cd, de}. For the partial graph X_U: how many vertices, how many edges, how many connected components?
Show solution

(a) Edges with both ends in the set: de, ef, eg, fh, gh, so 5 edges. Degrees inside: d:1, e:3, f:2, g:2, h:2. Sum 10 = 2·5.

(b) d(d) = 3, so X − d has 11 − 3 = 8 edges.

(c) A partial graph keeps every vertex: 10 vertices and 4 edges. The path a-b-c-d-e is one component, and f, g, h, i, j are 5 single-vertex components. Total 6 components.

Chains and cycles (Chaînes et cycles)

↑ top

Lesson sections 4 and 5 · uses graph X

2.1Standard
For each sequence in X, decide whether it is a chain. If it is, give its length and say whether it is elementary, simple, both or neither. For elementary chains, list the chords.
  1. (a, b, d, e, f, h)
  2. (b, a, c, b, d)
  3. (e, f, h, g, e, f)
  4. (a, b, e)
  5. (b, d, c, a, b) starts and ends at b. Is it a cycle? Give its length and parity.
Show solution

(a) A chain of length 5. All vertices are distinct, so it is elementary (hence simple too). No two non-consecutive vertices are adjacent, so there is no chord: it is an induced chain.

(b) A chain of length 4. The edges ba, ac, cb, bd are all different, so it is simple. Vertex b appears twice, so it is not elementary.

(c) A chain of length 5, but the edge ef is used twice, so it is not simple and not elementary: neither.

(d) Not a chain: be is not an edge of X.

(e) Yes. It is a simple chain of length 4 whose ends coincide, so a cycle. Its length is 4 (even), and b, d, c, a are distinct, so it is an elementary cycle.

2.2Standard
Find an elementary chain from a to h in X that has no chord, and give its length. Then compute d(a, h) and explain why no chain from a to h can be shorter.
Show solution

(a, b, d, e, f, h) has length 5 and no chord. Another one: (a, c, d, e, g, h).

Layers from a: {b, c, i} at distance 1, {d} at 2, {e} at 3, {f, g} at 4, {h} at 5. So d(a, h) = 5. The distance is by definition the length of a shortest chain, and h first appears in layer 5, so nothing shorter exists.

2.3Standard
List all elementary cycles of X that pass through b, with their lengths. Which are odd? Is there a cycle that avoids b? Give it.
Show solution

Through b: (a, b, c) of length 3 (odd), (b, c, d) of length 3 (odd), and (a, b, d, c) of length 4 (even).

X has exactly four elementary cycles in total. The fourth avoids b: (e, f, h, g), length 4.

2.4Standard
For each graph decide whether it has a Hamiltonian cycle and whether it has an Eulerian cycle. Give the cycle or a short reason.
  1. K5
  2. K_{2,3}
  3. the windmill: three triangles sharing one vertex
  4. the diamond: the square pqrs plus the diagonal pr
Show solution

(a) Both. Hamiltonian: 1-2-3-4-5-1. Eulerian: connected and every degree is 4 (even).

(b) Neither. A Hamiltonian cycle alternates between the two sides, so the sides would need equal size, but they have 2 and 3 vertices. Degrees are 3, 3, 2, 2, 2, and odd degrees rule out an Eulerian cycle.

(c) Eulerian only. All degrees are even (6 at the centre, 2 elsewhere), e.g. c→o1→o2→c→o3→o4→c→o5→o6→c. Not Hamiltonian: removing a vertex from a Hamiltonian cycle leaves a connected path, but removing the centre disconnects the graph.

(d) Hamiltonian only: p-q-r-s-p. Vertices p and r have degree 3 (odd), so no Eulerian cycle.

2.5Warm-up
Is the cycle (e, f, h, g, e) of X even or odd? Is it elementary? Does it have a chord?
Show solution

Length 4, so even. The vertices e, f, h, g are distinct, so it is elementary. The non-consecutive pairs e–h and f–g are not edges, so it has no chord.

Distance and connectivity (Distance et connexité)

↑ top

Lesson sections 6 and 7 · uses graph X

3.1Standard
X has an isolated vertex, so distances to j are infinite. Work in X − j, which is connected.
  1. Compute the distance from a to every vertex.
  2. Compute exc(a), exc(d) and exc(h).
  3. Find diam(X − j) and a pair of vertices that reaches it.
Show solution

(a) b, c, i: 1. d: 2. e: 3. f, g: 4. h: 5.

(b) exc(a) = 5 (farthest is h). exc(d) = 3 (farthest are i and h). exc(h) = 6 (farthest is i, via h-f-e-d-b-a-i).

(c) All eccentricities: a:5, b:4, c:4, d:3, e:4, f:5, g:5, h:6, i:6. So diam = 6, reached by the pair (h, i).

3.2Standard
In X − j, compute B_1(d), B_2(d) and B_3(d). Compare B_1(d) with N[d]. What is the smallest r with B_r(d) equal to the whole vertex set, and how does it relate to exc(d)?
Show solution

B_1(d) = {b, c, d, e} = N[d]. For r = 1 the ball is the closed neighbourhood.

B_2(d) = {a, b, c, d, e, f, g}: it adds a (via b), and f, g (via e).

B_3(d) adds i (via a) and h (via f), so it is all 9 vertices. The smallest such r is 3, which equals exc(d). In general B_r(v) is everything exactly when r ≥ exc(v).

3.3Standard
In X:
  1. What are the connected components of X?
  2. Is X − d connected? Give its components.
  3. What is the least number of edges to add to X to make it connected? Give one choice.
  4. Is X − j connected?
Show solution

(a) Two components: {a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h, i} and {j}.

(b) No. Its components are {a, b, c, i}, {e, f, g, h} and {j}. Vertex d was the only link between the left and right parts.

(c) One edge, for example ja. With c components you need at least c − 1 edges, here 2 − 1 = 1.

(d) Yes: a search from a reaches all 9 remaining vertices.

Complement, cliques and stable sets (Complémentaire, cliques et stables)

↑ top

Lesson section 8 · uses graph W

4.1Warm-up
In W:
  1. How many edges has W?
  2. List the edges of the complement W̄ and check that its size is C(7,2) − m.
  3. What are the complements of K6 and of K_{3,3}?
Show solution

(a) 6 edges with |i − j| = 1 and 5 edges with |i − j| = 2: m = 11.

(b) Pairs with |i − j| ≥ 3: 14, 15, 16, 17, 25, 26, 27, 36, 37, 47. That is 10 edges, and C(7,2) − 11 = 21 − 11 = 10. ✓

(c) The complement of K6 has no edges at all (6 isolated vertices, the stable graph S6). The complement of K_{3,3} joins the pairs inside each side, giving two disjoint triangles.

4.2Standard
Find ω(W) and α(W), and prove that each value cannot be improved.
Show solution

Clique: {1, 2, 3} is a clique, so ω ≥ 3. In any clique, all indices differ by at most 2, so they lie in a window of 3 consecutive numbers: at most 3 vertices. Hence ω(W) = 3.

Stable set: {1, 4, 7} has pairwise gaps of 3, so it is stable and α ≥ 3. The vertices are covered by the 3 cliques {1,2,3}, {4,5,6}, {7}, and a stable set takes at most one vertex from each clique. Hence α(W) = 3.

4.3Standard
In W:
  1. Find a maximal stable set of size 2 and show that it is maximal.
  2. Give i(W).
  3. Is every maximal stable set maximum?
Show solution

(a) {3, 6}: the vertices 3 and 6 are not adjacent. N(3) = {1,2,4,5} and N(6) = {4,5,7}, so together with 3 and 6 every vertex is accounted for. Nothing can be added, so it is maximal.

(b) i(W) = 2. A single vertex is never maximal here: the largest degree is 4, below 6, so every vertex misses someone who could join it.

(c) No. {3, 6} is maximal but has size 2, while α(W) = 3.

4.4Standard
Without redrawing anything:
  1. Give ω(W̄) and α(W̄).
  2. A graph has 10 vertices and 18 edges. How many edges has its complement?
  3. A graph G has a clique of size 4 and a stable set of size 5. What do they become in Ḡ?
Show solution

(a) A clique of W is a stable set of W̄ and vice versa, so ω(W̄) = α(W) = 3 and α(W̄) = ω(W) = 3.

(b) C(10,2) − 18 = 45 − 18 = 27.

(c) The clique of size 4 becomes a stable set of size 4, and the stable set of size 5 becomes a clique of size 5.

Modelling with graphs (Modélisation)

↑ top

Lesson section 9 style: turn a situation into a graph

5.1Standard
Eight guests A to H. An edge joins two guests who dislike each other.
  1. What is the largest group in which nobody dislikes anybody? Prove that no larger group exists.
  2. The host seats guests at tables so that nobody shares a table with someone they dislike. What is the fewest tables needed? Give a seating.
Show solution

(a) α = 3, for example {A, D, G}. The triangles {A,B,C} and {D,E,F} and the edge {G,H} are three cliques covering all 8 guests. A group of mutually friendly guests has at most one guest per clique, so at most 3.

(b) 3 tables. Each triangle needs 3 different tables, so at least 3. The seating {A, D, G}, {B, E, H}, {C, F} has no disliking pair inside a table. So χ = 3.

5.2Standard
Six exams M, P, C, B, E, L. Two exams conflict when a student takes both: M–P, M–C, P–C, C–B, B–E, E–L, L–B. Exams in the same slot must not conflict.
  1. Model this as a graph and say what a time slot corresponds to.
  2. What is the minimum number of slots? Give a timetable.
  3. What is the largest number of exams that can share one slot?
Show solution

(a) Vertices are exams, edges are conflicts. A slot is a set of pairwise non-conflicting exams, that is a stable set; a timetable is a proper colouring with one colour per slot.

(b) M, P, C form a triangle, so at least 3 slots. The timetable {M, B}, {P, E}, {C, L} works, so the minimum is 3.

(c) {M, P, C} and {B, E, L} are two triangles covering all six exams, so a slot holds at most 2 exams. {M, B} shows 2 is reachable.

Matchings, covers and the four identities (Couplages, transversaux, recouvrements)

↑ top

Lesson sections 10 to 12 · uses the ladder L

6.1Warm-up
In L:
  1. Find a perfect matching.
  2. Why is it also a maximum matching, and what is the maximum size of a matching?
Show solution

(a) The four rungs {a1b1, a2b2, a3b3, a4b4} touch every vertex exactly once.

(b) Each edge saturates 2 vertices, so a matching on 8 vertices has at most 8/2 = 4 edges. A perfect matching reaches this bound, so the maximum size is 4.

6.2Standard
Find a maximal matching of L that is not maximum. Which vertices does it leave unsaturated?
Show solution

M = {a1a2, a3a4, b2b3} has 3 edges and saturates a1, a2, a3, a4, b2, b3.

Unsaturated: b1 and b4. The neighbours of b1 are a1, b2 (both saturated), and the neighbours of b4 are a4, b3 (both saturated). So no edge can be added: M is maximal. But 3 is less than 4, so it is not maximum.

6.3Standard
For L, compute α(L), the minimum size of a transversal, the maximum size of a matching and the minimum size of an edge cover. Then verify Properties 1 to 4.
Show solution

α(L) = 4, for example the stable set {a1, a3, b2, b4}.

By Property 1 the complement {a2, a4, b1, b3} is a transversal, so min|T| ≤ 4; and max|S| + min|T| = 8 gives exactly 4. Check: every edge has an end in it.

Maximum matching: 4 (the rungs). Minimum edge cover: 8 − 4 = 4 by Property 3, and the four rungs are one.

Property 2: 4 + 4 = 8. Property 3: 4 + 4 = 8. Property 4: L is bipartite, so max|M| = min|T| = 4 and max|S| = min|R| = 4. ✓

6.4Standard
Repeat the computation for the 5-cycle C5. Which property fails, and why?
Show solution

n = 5. Maximum matching: 2. Stable number: α = 2. Minimum transversal: 5 − 2 = 3. Minimum edge cover: 5 − 2 = 3.

Property 2: 2 + 3 = 5 ✓. Property 3: 2 + 3 = 5 ✓.

Property 4 fails: max|M| = 2 but min|T| = 3. It only holds for bipartite graphs, and C5 has an odd cycle, so it is not bipartite.

6.5Challenge
Find a transversal of L that is minimal but not minimum. Hint: use Property 1 on a maximal stable set that is not maximum.
Show solution

S = {a1, a4, b2} is stable and maximal: the neighbours of its vertices are a2, b1, a3, b4, b3, which are all the other vertices. It has size 3, below α = 4.

Its complement T = {a2, a3, b1, b3, b4} is a transversal (Property 1) of size 5. It is minimal: remove any vertex x of T and x joins S back, but S ∪ {x} is not stable, so x has an edge to S that is now uncovered.

It is not minimum, since the minimum size is 4.

6.6Warm-up
A graph has 9 vertices.
  1. At most how many vertices can a matching saturate?
  2. Can it have a perfect matching?
  3. What is the smallest possible size of an edge cover?
Show solution

(a) Each edge saturates 2 vertices, so with 4 edges at most 8 vertices.

(b) No. A perfect matching would need 9/2 edges.

(c) At least ⌈9/2⌉ = 5: each edge covers at most 2 vertices. (By Property 3 it is exactly 9 − max|M|, which is 5 when a matching of size 4 exists.)

6.7Standard
True or false? Give a counterexample when false.
  1. Every maximum matching is maximal.
  2. Every maximal matching is maximum.
  3. Every perfect matching is maximum.
  4. Every minimal transversal is minimum.
  5. Every minimum transversal is minimal.
Show solution

(a) True: if an edge could be added, the matching would not have been of maximum size.

(b) False: {a1a2, a3a4, b2b3} in L is maximal of size 3, but the maximum is 4.

(c) True: it has n/2 edges, the most any matching can have.

(d) False: {a2, a3, b1, b3, b4} in L is minimal of size 5, but the minimum is 4.

(e) True: removing a vertex from a minimum transversal would give a smaller one.

Regular, bipartite and tree-like graphs (Réguliers, bipartis, arbres)

↑ top

Lesson sections 13 to 15

7.1Warm-up
Answer using m = n·k/2 for a k-regular graph.
  1. How many edges has a 4-regular graph on 9 vertices?
  2. Is there a 3-regular graph on 7 vertices?
  3. Give a 3-regular graph on 6 vertices.
  4. Give the degree and number of edges of K9.
Show solution

(a) 9·4/2 = 18.

(b) No: the degrees would sum to 7·3 = 21, which is odd, but the degree sum is always 2m, an even number.

(c) K_{3,3} or the triangular prism (two triangles joined by three parallel edges).

(d) 8-regular, with 9·8/2 = 36 edges.

7.2Warm-up
Give the order and size of K_{4,6}, K_{5,5}, K_{2,3,4} and K_{1,7}. Which of them is regular? Which is a star?
Show solution

K_{4,6}: order 10, size 4·6 = 24.

K_{5,5}: order 10, size 25, and it is 5-regular (K_{r,r} has order 2r and size r²).

K_{2,3,4}: order 9, size 2·3 + 2·4 + 3·4 = 26. Check: C(9,2) − (1 + 3 + 6) = 36 − 10 = 26.

K_{1,7}: order 8, size 7, and it is the star.

Only K_{5,5} is regular.

7.3Standard
Both graphs are an 8-cycle 1-2-…-8-1 plus one chord (orange). Use the odd-cycle test to decide which is bipartite. If bipartite, give the teams; otherwise give an odd cycle.
Show solution

Graph 1 is not bipartite: (1, 2, 3, 4, 5) closed by the chord 1–5 is a cycle of length 5, which is odd.

Graph 2 is bipartite, with teams {1, 3, 5, 7} and {2, 4, 6, 8}. The cycle edges join odd to even labels, and so does the chord 1–4. The cycles through the chord have lengths 4 (1,2,3,4) and 6 (1,4,5,6,7,8), both even.

7.4Standard
Trees and forests.
  1. A forest has 20 vertices and 14 edges. How many trees does it have?
  2. A tree has 4 vertices of degree 3, one vertex of degree 2, and all other vertices are leaves. How many vertices does it have?
  3. Why does every tree with at least 2 vertices have at least 2 leaves?
Show solution

(a) A forest with c trees has n − c edges, so 14 = 20 − c and c = 6.

(b) Let ℓ be the number of leaves, so n = 5 + ℓ. A tree has n − 1 edges and the degrees sum to 2(n − 1): 4·3 + 2 + ℓ = 2(4 + ℓ), so 14 + ℓ = 8 + 2ℓ and ℓ = 6. Hence n = 11. Check: 10 edges, degree sum 12 + 2 + 6 = 20. ✓

(c) Take a longest elementary chain. Its end vertex has no neighbour outside the chain (the chain could be extended) and no second neighbour inside it (that would close a cycle). So the end has degree 1. Both ends give 2 leaves.

7.5Standard
The Petersen graph (outer vertices 0–4, inner vertices 5–9).
  1. Give its order, size and degree.
  2. Is it bipartite? If not, exhibit an odd cycle.
  3. Find a stable set of size 4.
Show solution

(a) Order 10, 3-regular, so size 10·3/2 = 15.

(b) Not bipartite: the outer cycle (0, 1, 2, 3, 4) has length 5, which is odd.

(c) {0, 2, 8, 9}: 0 and 2 are not adjacent, and neither is adjacent to 8 or 9; and 8, 9 are not adjacent. (The maximum is 4.)

Closures and hypergraphs (Fermetures et hypergraphes)

↑ top

Lesson sections 16 and 17

8.1Standard
Let G be the path P6 on vertices 1, 2, …, 6. Count the edges of G, G², G³, G⁴, G⁵. From which t is G^t complete?
Show solution

Pairs at distance k in a path of 6 vertices: 6 − k. So the counts are cumulative: G¹: 5, G²: 5 + 4 = 9, G³: 9 + 3 = 12, G⁴: 12 + 2 = 14, G⁵: 14 + 1 = 15.

15 = C(6,2), so G^t is complete from t = 5, which is diam(G).

8.2Standard
Draw G² for the 6-cycle C6. How many edges does it have and which graph is it?
Show solution

Pairs at distance 1: 6, at distance 2: 6, at distance 3: 3 (the opposite pairs). So G² has 6 + 6 = 12 edges.

The only non-adjacent pairs are the opposite ones {1,4}, {2,5}, {3,6}. So G² = K_{2,2,2}, the octahedron, with these three pairs as its parts.

8.3Standard
A contact network (figure). Answer with the closure G².
  1. Who are Bea's direct contacts?
  2. Who are Bea's direct and indirect contacts, that is N_{G²}(Bea)?
  3. Who is not within G² of Ali?
  4. How many edges have G and G²?
Show solution

(a) {Ali, Cal, Dan}.

(b) Add contacts of contacts: Eve (via Cal) and Fay (via Dan). So N_{G²}(Bea) = {Ali, Cal, Dan, Eve, Fay}.

(c) N_{G²}(Ali) = {Bea, Cal, Dan}, so Eve, Fay, Gus are not reached.

(d) G has 6 edges. New pairs at distance 2: Ali–Cal, Ali–Dan, Cal–Dan, Bea–Eve, Bea–Fay, Dan–Gus, which is 6 more. So G² has 12 edges.

8.4Standard
Read the hypergraph in the figure.
  1. Write V and E, and give the size of each hyperedge.
  2. For each vertex, count the hyperedges that contain it. Which vertex is in none?
  3. Which hyperedges behave like ordinary graph edges?
  4. Why can an ordinary graph be seen as a hypergraph?
Show solution

(a) V = {1, …, 8} and E = {A, B, C, D} with A = {1,2,3} (size 3), B = {3,4} (size 2), C = {4,5,6} (size 3), D = {7} (size 1).

(b) 1:1, 2:1, 3:2, 4:2, 5:1, 6:1, 7:1, 8:0. The total is 9 = 3 + 2 + 3 + 1. Vertex 8 is in no hyperedge.

(c) Only B, since it has exactly 2 vertices.

(d) An ordinary edge xy is a subset {x, y} of V of size 2, so a graph is a hypergraph whose hyperedges all have size 2.

Homomorphisms, colouring and isomorphism (Homomorphismes, coloration, isomorphisme)

↑ top

Lesson sections 21 to 23

9.1Warm-up
Homomorphisms into complete graphs.
  1. Is f: C6 → K2 defined by f(i) = i mod 2 (vertices 1..6 around the cycle) a homomorphism?
  2. Show that no homomorphism C5 → K2 exists.
  3. Give a homomorphism C5 → K3 and say what it is as a colouring.
Show solution

(a) Yes. Consecutive vertices i, i+1 have different parities, and 6 and 1 do too, so every edge goes to the edge {0,1} of K2.

(b) Adjacent vertices must have different images, and K2 has only two vertices, so images alternate around the cycle. After 5 steps vertex 5 would have the same image as vertex 1, but they are adjacent. Impossible.

(c) f(1..5) = 1, 2, 1, 2, 3. It is a proper 3-colouring of C5: a homomorphism into K3 is exactly a 3-colouring.

9.2Standard
Find χ for each graph and justify with a colouring or a reason.
  1. the ladder L
  2. the graph W of set 4
  3. the wheel (a hub w joined to every vertex of a 5-cycle, see figure)
  4. C7
  5. K_{3,3}
Show solution

(a) χ = 2: bipartite. Colour a1, a3, b2, b4 with 1 and a2, a4, b1, b3 with 2.

(b) χ = 3: the triangle {1,2,3} forces 3 colours, and colouring vertex i with i mod 3 works because adjacent vertices differ by 1 or 2.

(c) χ = 4: the rim C5 is an odd cycle and needs 3 colours, and the hub touches all rim vertices, so it needs a fourth. Rim 1,2,1,2,3, hub 4.

(d) χ = 3: an odd cycle cannot use 2 colours; 1,2,1,2,1,2,3 works.

(e) χ = 2: bipartite, one colour per side.

9.3Standard
For each graph state what Brooks' theorem says about χ, then compare with the true value: C7, K6, the cube Q3, the Petersen graph, the wheel from the previous problem.
Show solution

C7: odd cycle, Δ = 2, so χ = Δ + 1 = 3. True value 3.

K6: complete graph, Δ = 5, so χ = Δ + 1 = 6. True value 6.

Q3: connected, not a clique or odd cycle, Δ = 3, so χ ≤ 3. True value 2 (it is bipartite): the bound is not tight.

Petersen: Δ = 3, so χ ≤ 3. True value 3 (it has a 5-cycle): tight.

Wheel: Δ = 5, so χ ≤ 5. True value 4: Brooks gives only an upper bound.

9.4Warm-up
Graph G1 has 7 vertices with degrees (3,3,2,2,2,2,2). Graph G2 has 7 vertices with degrees (4,2,2,2,2,2,2).
  1. Can they be isomorphic?
  2. If two graphs had the same degree sequence, would that prove they are isomorphic?
Show solution

(a) No. Both have 16/2 = 8 edges, but the lists of degrees differ, and isomorphic graphs must have the same degree list.

(b) No. Equal degree sequences are necessary but not sufficient. The next problem is a counterexample.

9.5Standard
The triangular prism and K_{3,3} are both 3-regular with 6 vertices and 9 edges. Are they isomorphic?
Show solution

No. The prism contains a triangle (1, 2, 3), so it has an odd cycle and is not bipartite. K_{3,3} is bipartite. One differing property is enough: they also differ in ω (3 versus 2) and χ (3 versus 2).

9.6Standard
G1 is the 6-cycle 1-2-3-4-5-6-1 plus the chord 14. G2 has edges pq, px, xy, yq, pz, zw, wq. Find an isomorphism f: G1 → G2 and check all 7 edges. Hint: match the two degree-3 vertices first.
Show solution

The degree-3 vertices are 1, 4 in G1 and p, q in G2, and they are adjacent in both. Take f(1) = p, f(4) = q, then the path 1-2-3-4 goes to p-x-y-q and the path 1-6-5-4 to p-z-w-q: f(2) = x, f(3) = y, f(6) = z, f(5) = w.

Edges: 12 → px, 23 → xy, 34 → yq, 45 → qw, 56 → wz, 61 → zp, 14 → pq. All 7 are edges of G2, and G2 has exactly 7 edges, so f preserves adjacency in both directions.

9.7Standard
Draw all graphs, up to isomorphism, with 4 vertices and 3 edges. Justify that you have them all.
Show solution

The degrees sum to 6. The possible degree lists are (3,1,1,1), (2,2,1,1) and (2,2,2,0); (3,2,1,0) is impossible because a vertex of degree 3 touches all others, so none is isolated.

(3,1,1,1): the star K_{1,3}. (2,2,1,1): the path P4. (2,2,2,0): a triangle plus an isolated vertex. These three are pairwise non-isomorphic, since their degree lists differ. So there are exactly 3.

10.1Standard
Let G = K2 with edge uv and H = K3 with triangle 1, 2, 3. For each of G × H, G □ H, G ⊗ H, G ⊠ H: list the edges, count them and name the graph.
Show solution

Direct: (u,i) ~ (v,j) with i ≠ j: u1v2, u1v3, u2v1, u2v3, u3v1, u3v2. 6 edges forming a 6-cycle C6.

Cartesian: a triangle on the u copies (3), a triangle on the v copies (3) and the rungs u1v1, u2v2, u3v3 (3). 9 edges: the triangular prism.

Lexicographic: since uv is an edge, all 3·3 = 9 pairs u_i v_j are adjacent, plus the triangles inside each copy (3 + 3). 15 edges = C(6,2): K6.

Total: Cartesian 9 + direct 6 = 15 edges: also K6.

10.2Challenge
Derive the number of edges of each product in terms of n_G, m_G, n_H, m_H, check your formulas on problem 1, then evaluate them for G = P4 and H = P3.
Show solution

Direct: each pair (edge of G, edge of H) gives 2 edges, so 2·m_G·m_H.

Cartesian: n_G copies of H and n_H copies of G, so n_G·m_H + n_H·m_G. Total: Cartesian + direct.

Lexicographic: each edge of G joins all n_H² pairs of copies, plus the edges inside the n_G copies of H: m_G·n_H² + n_G·m_H.

Check with K2, K3: direct 2·1·3 = 6, Cartesian 2·3 + 3·1 = 9, lexicographic 1·9 + 2·3 = 15, total 9 + 6 = 15. ✓

P4 (n=4, m=3) and P3 (n=3, m=2): direct 12, Cartesian 4·2 + 3·3 = 17, total 17 + 12 = 29, lexicographic 3·9 + 4·2 = 35.

10.3Standard
Check the formulas χ(G × H) = min{χ(G), χ(H)} and χ(G □ H) = max{χ(G), χ(H)} for G = K3 and H = K2.
Show solution

K3 × K2 is a 6-cycle (as in problem 1), so χ = 2 = min{3, 2}. ✓

K3 □ K2 is the triangular prism: it contains a triangle, so χ ≥ 3, and colouring both triangles 1, 2, 3 in matching order works. So χ = 3 = max{3, 2}. ✓

10.4Standard
Hypercube Q4.
  1. Give its number of vertices, the degree and the number of edges.
  2. Compute the distance between 0110 and 1011 and give a shortest path.
  3. How many vertices are at distance exactly 2 from 0000?
  4. Why is every Q_n bipartite? Give the teams.
  5. What is the diameter of Q_n?
Show solution

(a) 2⁴ = 16 vertices, each of degree 4, so 16·4/2 = 32 edges (n·2^{n−1}).

(b) They differ in positions 1, 2 and 4, so the distance is 3. Path: 0110 → 1110 → 1010 → 1011 (flip position 1, then 2, then 4).

(c) Choose which 2 of the 4 bits to flip: C(4,2) = 6.

(d) Flipping one bit changes the number of 1s by exactly 1, so every edge joins an even-weight word to an odd-weight word. Teams: even weight and odd weight. So there are no odd cycles.

(e) n: the distance between a word and its complement is n, and no two words differ in more than n places.

10.5Standard
Hamming graphs H(d, q) (Cartesian product of d copies of K_q).
  1. For H(2,4): vertices, degree, edges, diameter.
  2. The same for H(3,3).
  3. Which H(d, q) is the hypercube Q5?
  4. Is H(2,3) = H(3,2)?
  5. Why does H(2,3) have 18 edges while the 3×3 grid has 12?
Show solution

(a) 4² = 16 vertices, degree d(q−1) = 2·3 = 6, edges 16·6/2 = 48, diameter 2 (two coordinates).

(b) 27 vertices, degree 3·2 = 6, edges 27·6/2 = 81, diameter 3.

(c) H(5, 2).

(d) No: H(2,3) has 3² = 9 vertices, H(3,2) has 2³ = 8 and equals Q3.

(e) In H(2,3) every row and every column is a triangle (K3, 3 edges), and there are 3 rows + 3 columns: 6·3 = 18. In the grid they are paths (2 edges): 6·2 = 12.

10.6Standard
Grids and tori.
  1. The 4×5 grid: vertices, edges, the Manhattan distance between (1,1) and (4,5), and the diameter.
  2. The torus C4 □ C5: vertices, degree, edges, the Lee distance between (1,1) and (3,4), and the diameter.
  3. Give the possible vertex degrees in the strip S_3 (3 rows, infinite both ways) and the half-strip S_3^+ (3 rows, columns 0, 1, 2, …).
Show solution

(a) 20 vertices. Edges: 4·4 + 5·3 = 16 + 15 = 31. Distance: |4−1| + |5−1| = 3 + 4 = 7, which is also the diameter (k−1) + (n−1).

(b) 20 vertices, degree 2 + 2 = 4, edges 20·4/2 = 40. Lee distance: row gap min(2, 4−2) = 2, column gap min(3, 5−3) = 2, total 4. Diameter ⌊4/2⌋ + ⌊5/2⌋ = 2 + 2 = 4.

(c) S_3: top and bottom rows have degree 3 (left, right, one vertical), the middle row has degree 4. S_3^+: the corner vertices of column 0 have degree 2, the middle vertex of column 0 has degree 3, the top and bottom rows elsewhere 3, the middle row elsewhere 4.

11.2Warm-up
Binary words u = (1,1,0,1,0,0,1) and v = (0,1,1,1,0,1,0). Compute u + v (sum mod 2), relate its number of 1s to the distance, and compute all three metrics.
Show solution

u + v = (1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 1): add each position with 0+0=0, 0+1=1, 1+1=0.

It has four 1s, and the Hamming distance is exactly the number of positions where the words differ, so 4.

On a binary alphabet Hamming, Manhattan and Lee all equal 4, since each differing position contributes 1 in every metric.

11.3Standard
Vertices are the words of length k over an alphabet of l letters, with an edge when the distance is 1. For each case say which graph each metric gives and count its edges.
  1. l = 5, k = 1
  2. l = 3, k = 2
  3. l = 4, k = 2
  4. In which case do two metrics give the same graph, and why?
Show solution

(a) Hamming: K5 (10 edges). Manhattan: the path P5 (4 edges). Lee: the cycle C5 (5 edges).

(b) 9 vertices. Hamming: K3 □ K3 (18 edges). Manhattan: the grid P3 □ P3 (12 edges). Lee: C3 □ C3 (18 edges).

(c) 16 vertices. Hamming: K4 □ K4 (48 edges). Manhattan: the grid P4 □ P4 (24 edges). Lee: the torus C4 □ C4 (32 edges).

(d) In case (b), Lee and Hamming agree, because C3 = K3: with 3 letters on a circle every pair of letters is at distance 1. With 2 letters all three metrics agree and the graph is the hypercube.

12.1Challenge
Let G be a connected 2-regular graph on 5 vertices. Show that G is the cycle C5, and that G is isomorphic to its complement.
Show solution

A connected graph where every vertex has degree 2 is a single cycle through all vertices, so G = C5.

In the complement each vertex has degree 4 − 2 = 2, so the complement is 2-regular on 5 vertices. On vertices 1..5 the complement of C5 has edges 13, 35, 52, 24, 41, which is the cycle (1, 3, 5, 2, 4). The map f(i) = 2i mod 5 sends the cycle 1-2-3-4-5 to this one, so C5 ≅ C̄5.

12.2Challenge
A bipartite graph has 12 vertices and a perfect matching. Find the maximum matching size, the minimum transversal size, α and the minimum edge cover size.
Show solution

A perfect matching on 12 vertices has 6 edges, which is the maximum: max|M| = 6.

Property 4 (bipartite): min|T| = max|M| = 6.

Property 1 and 2: α = 12 − 6 = 6.

Minimum edge cover: 12 − 6 = 6 by Property 3 (the perfect matching itself is one).

12.3Challenge
Prove that in every graph the number of vertices of odd degree is even.
Show solution

The degrees add up to 2m, an even number. The vertices of even degree contribute an even amount, so the vertices of odd degree must also add up to an even number. A sum of odd numbers is even only if there is an even number of terms.

12.4Challenge
Prove that if G is disconnected, its complement Ḡ is connected.
Show solution

Take two vertices u, v. If they are in different components of G, they are not adjacent in G, so they are adjacent in Ḡ.

If they are in the same component, pick a vertex w from another component. Then w is adjacent to neither u nor v in G, so u – w – v is a chain in Ḡ. Hence any two vertices are connected in Ḡ.

12.5Challenge
Show that every tree is bipartite. For the path P6 (vertices 1..6) give the teams, the maximum matching, α, the minimum transversal and the minimum edge cover, and check Properties 3 and 4.
Show solution

A tree has no cycle at all, so no odd cycle, so it is bipartite (odd-cycle test).

Teams: {1, 3, 5} and {2, 4, 6}.

Maximum matching: {12, 34, 56}, size 3 (perfect). α = 3 (for example {1, 3, 5}). Minimum transversal: {2, 4, 6}, size 3. Minimum edge cover: the same three edges, size 3.

Property 3: 3 + 3 = 6 = n. ✓ Property 4: max|M| = min|T| = 3 and max|S| = min|R| = 3. ✓
