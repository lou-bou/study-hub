1

Why monitor? (Constat)

↑ contents
In plain words

In tech you must keep up with innovations. But the useful news is buried under useless or repeated news, and you cannot spend unlimited time searching. So you need a system that collects the relevant information for you, automatically and all the time. That system is monitoring.

  • monitoring (veille)From "être éveillé": staying awake and attentive to everything happening in your information environment.
  • technology monitoring (veille technologique)Monitoring done with technological tools for collecting, processing and distributing information.databases, RSS feed aggregators (agrégateurs de flux RSS), alert services (services d'alerte)
  • relevant information (information pertinente)The small part of what is published that actually matters for your decisions.
Worked example

Apply the chain to you, a Big Data Analytics student who wants to follow new data tools.

  • Fact: new frameworks, papers and releases appear every week.
  • But: most posts are hype, reposts or off-topic.
  • And: you have courses; you cannot read everything by hand.
  • So: subscribe to a few chosen sources through an RSS aggregator and set alerts on 3 or 4 keywords. The tools collect; you only read what passes the filter. That is monitoring, not occasional searching.
2

Three definitions (Définitions de la veille)

↑ contents
In plain words

All three definitions agree that monitoring is continuous and aimed at the future. Each stresses a different thing: AFNOR the goal (watch and anticipate), ADBS the organisation (a structured setup), and the professionals the delivery (regular, tailored information for one recipient).

  • AFNOR (Association Française de Normalisation)The French standards body. Its definition comes from experimental standard XP X 50-053.
  • ADBS (Association des professionnels de l'information et de la documentation)The French association of information and documentation professionals.
  • information search (recherche d'information)A one-off activity that answers a precise need. Monitoring, by contrast, is continuous and organised.
  • monitoring service (prestation de veille)Monitoring delivered to a client: periodic, personalised, processed for that client's purpose.
SourceMonitoring is…What it stresses
AFNORa "continuous, largely iterative activity aiming at active surveillance of the technological, commercial… environment, to anticipate its evolutions"The goal: watch and detect changes. Unlike information search, it is continuous and anticipates.
ADBSan "organised, integrated and purposeful setup (dispositif) for collecting, processing, distributing and exploiting information, so an organisation can react in the medium and long term to changes or threats"The organisation of the setup, usually run by information professionals.
Professionals
ADBS, SCIP, SYNAPI, 2001		a "continuous, dynamic process of personalised, periodic delivery of processed data or information, with expertise in the subject"The delivery: regular and personalised, for a precise recipient.
Worked example

Monitoring or information search? Decide, then name the definition that fits best.

  1. "Find out this afternoon which Python library reads Parquet files." One precise need, answered once. information search
  2. "Every Monday, send the R&D team a short digest of new papers on stream processing." Continuous, periodic, personalised for one recipient. monitoring, the professionals' definition.
  3. "Set up a team, sources and procedure to watch competitors' patents, so the company can react over the next years." An organised setup, aimed at medium and long-term reaction. monitoring, the ADBS definition.

The course quiz (slides 6 to 8) checks exactly this: AFNOR = continuous, iterative surveillance; ADBS = organised, integrated, purposeful setup; professionals = regular, personalised delivery to a precise recipient.

3

Seven types of monitoring (Types de veille)

↑ contents
In plain words

Each type watches a different part of the company's surroundings: rivals, trading partners, the market, technology, the law, or the brand's image online. Strategic monitoring sits on top: it brings all of them together and turns them into action.

  • competitive (veille concurrentielle)Competitors direct and indirect, current and potential: prices, suppliers, strategy, hiring, communication.
  • commercial (veille commerciale)Clients and prospects, suppliers, distributors: contracts, new products. Helps find prospects and buy better.
  • marketing (veille marketing)The market, consumer behaviour, companies' communication, results of your own campaigns.
  • technological (veille technologique)Standards (normes) and technologies: patent filings (dépôts de brevets), new processes.
  • legal (veille juridique)Legislation, case law (jurisprudence), counterfeits (contrefaçons). Keeps you compliant.
  • e-reputation (veille d'e-réputation)The image, positive or negative, a brand carries online. Goal: turn a "bad buzz" into a "good buzz".
  • strategic (veille stratégique)The global approach: federates all the other types, adds the strategic dimension, oriented toward action.
Commercial vs marketing (course slide 21). Commercial monitoring is operational: day-to-day buying and selling relations (e.g. a supplier lowers its prices). Marketing monitoring is strategic: the market and consumers, to shape the offer (e.g. spotting the organic trend and adapting). They complement each other.

4

What monitoring is for (À quoi sert la veille ?)

↑ contents
In plain words

Monitoring has four jobs: see where the market is going, keep an eye on competitors, follow technology before it leaves you behind, and spot threats and opportunities early enough to act. It does not remove risk; it lets you see it and manage it.

  • forecast the market (prévoir l'évolution du marché)Know clients' needs and consumption trends, and dare to see the market in a new way.
  • watch competitors (surveiller les concurrents)Who they are, their patents, finances, sales, strategy, across R&D, production, marketing, HR, finance and management.
  • follow technology (tenir compte de l'évolution technologique)Technologies are born, evolve, disappear and get replaced. Watch scientific publications and patents.
  • opportunity (opportunité)A new process, component, idea, company to buy, market to take, alliance to make. Not seized = left to others.
  • threat (menace)An unfavourable change in the environment that, without reaction, will erode profitability over time.
Worked example

Kodak (course slide 13 and quiz slide 20).

  • Situation: Kodak dominated photographic film.
  • Signal: digital photography was rising (the new curve above).
  • Mistake: it saw itself as a film company, not an imaging company, so it did not reposition.
  • Result: it collapsed against new technologies.
  • What strategic monitoring would have done: redefine the business as imaging and take the lead in digital photography.

Turning a threat into an opportunity (course slide 20).

  • Threat: sales drop because clients prefer more ecological products.
  • Marketing and competitive monitoring detect the trend early.
  • Action: develop recyclable products and communicate on the ecological approach.
  • Result: new clients and a better brand image.
True or false? (course slide 19)
  • "Competitive monitoring only looks at direct competitors." False: also indirect, current and potential ones.
  • "A company that skips technology monitoring can stay competitive in the long run." False: it risks missing major technological shifts.
  • "Monitoring eliminates all risk." False: it identifies risks so you can manage them proactively.
5

The SWOT matrix (Analyse SWOT / FFOM)

↑ contents
In plain words

SWOT sorts everything you know into four boxes using two questions. Is it inside the organisation (you control it) or outside (you don't)? Does it help or hurt? Inside and helpful = Strength; inside and harmful = Weakness; outside and helpful = Opportunity; outside and harmful = Threat.

  • SWOT (FFOM: Forces, Faiblesses, Opportunités, Menaces)A planning tool to understand the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats of a project or company. Commonly credited to Albert Humphrey (Stanford, 1960s–70s).
  • strengths (forces)Internal, positive, tangible or intangible attributes that give an advantage.well-known brand, ample funds, economies of scale, engaged staff
  • weaknesses (faiblesses)Internal traits that put you at a disadvantage. Controllable: minimise or remove them.limited funds, narrow product range, obsolete technology, weak marketing
  • opportunities (opportunités)External, attractive conditions you can exploit to become more profitable. Come from market, competition, industry/government, technology.fast market growth, complacent rivals, new uses for a product
  • threats (menaces)External elements, beyond your control, that put the mission or operations at risk.foreign competitors arrive, substitute products, more regulation, economic slowdown
Worked example

SWOT for the course's case: an Algerian tech start-up launching an IoT product for agriculture (slides 22 and 40). O and T come from the course; S and W are illustrative.

HelpfulHarmful
InternalS: team skilled in IoT and data; close to local farmers' needsW: limited funds; one product only; unknown brand
ExternalO: growing adoption of IoT sensors to optimise irrigationT: high equipment costs; network outages; regulation on IoT and data
  • Test each item: "Can we change it ourselves?" Yes → S or W. No → O or T.
  • "Network outages" is outside the start-up's control → T, even though it hurts the product directly.
  • "One product only" is the start-up's own choice → W, not T.
6

Running a SWOT (Comment mener l'analyse SWOT ?)

↑ contents
In plain words

First look around (inside and outside), then fill the four boxes and write them down, then decide what to do about them. Brainstorm freely, but keep only the five most important items per box. Treat the result as a guide, not a verdict: two people rarely produce the same SWOT.

  • brainstorming (remue-méninges)Start with four questions: What opportunities exist outside? What threats? What are our strengths? Our weaknesses?
  • prioritisation (priorisation)Cut S and W to at most five distinctive competencies and discouraging weaknesses; cut O and T to the five most important each.
  • action plan (plan d'action)The third step: what you will do with what the SWOT shows.
  • who uses it (qui en a besoin ?)Product launch, competitor or product evaluation, strategic planning, decision-making, workshops, brainstorming meetings, even a job change or personal development plan.
Do (faire)
  • Be analytical and specific.
  • Write down every thought and idea.
  • Be selective in the final evaluation.
  • Pick the right people, and one SWOT leader.
  • Be open to change.
Don't (ne pas faire)
  • Hide weaknesses.
  • Just list mistakes.
  • Lose sight of external influences and trends.
  • Turn it into a blame exercise.
  • Ignore the results later in planning.
Advantages (avantages)Pitfalls (pièges)
Helps set objectives for strategic planningVery subjective; use as a guide, not a prescription
A framework to identify and analyse S, W, O, TCan make things look too simple and miss key elements
A setting to analyse a situation and build strategiesClassifying is uncertain when the market is uncertain
A basis to assess capabilities and skillsMust be redone regularly; change is fast
A group exercise everyone can joinMay rest on assumptions that later prove wrong
Worked example

Course exercise (slides 41 to 44): University studies in the age of AI tools like ChatGPT: opportunity or threat?

  1. Locate the subject. We analyse university studies. ChatGPT comes from outside, so its effects go in O or T, not S or W.
  2. Brainstorm O. Fast access to information; help with research (ideas, summaries); writing assistance (content, grammar, structure); automation of tedious tasks.
  3. Brainstorm T. Plagiarism (copying AI text without attribution); unreliable or biased output; loss of essential thinking and research skills; ethics and intellectual property.
  4. Prioritise. 4 items in O and 4 in T, already within the limit of 5 per box.
  5. Action plan. Both: a valuable opportunity if used responsibly. Institutions and students should write ethical guidelines together, to maximise benefits and minimise risks.
7

Monitoring feeds SWOT (La veille dans l'analyse SWOT)

↑ contents
In plain words

A classic SWOT is a photo taken at one moment. Monitoring keeps filming the outside world, so new opportunities and threats show up in the O and T boxes before they become critical. That turns SWOT from a snapshot into a tool for anticipating.

  • early signal (indice précoce, signal faible)A small sign of a coming change: a patent, a trend, a draft regulation.
  • role of monitoring in SWOT (rôle de la veille)Identify threats and opportunities before they become critical. It does not replace SWOT and is not limited to internal weaknesses.
  • interaction between boxes (interaction entre les cases)A weakness can amplify a threat or block an opportunity; an opportunity you don't seize can become a threat.
Worked example

Course quiz (slide 38): a company lists "lack of artificial intelligence skills" as a weakness. Which consequences hold?

StatementVerdictReason
It can amplify a threat (rivals arrive using AI)yesW makes you vulnerable to T
It can stop it seizing an opportunity (new AI-related markets)yesW blocks O
It automatically becomes a threatnoW is internal, T is external; the box never changes
It can be offset by an opportunity, e.g. a partnership with a research instituteyesuse an O to fix a W

And the reverse link (slide 39): a new technology is an opportunity, but if the company does not act, competitors use it and it becomes a threat. opportunity missed → threat

Errors found in the original course
  • Slide 3: the AFNOR standard is written "XP X 50-53". Its reference is XP X 50-053 (AFNOR, 1998, on monitoring services).
  • SWOT slides 25 and 32: typos "Strenghts" → Strengths, "Oppurtunités / Oppurtunities" → Opportunités / Opportunities, "Effecuter" → Effectuer. Slide 28: "Ils doivent être minimisées" → Elles doivent être minimisées (les faiblesses).
  • Slide 13, Kodak: saying it "did not anticipate" digital is too strong. A Kodak engineer (Steven Sasson) built the first digital camera prototype in 1975. Kodak saw the technology but did not reposition around it, and filed for bankruptcy in 2012. The lesson is about acting on monitoring, not only doing it.
  • Slide 25: crediting SWOT to Albert Humphrey at Stanford is common but poorly documented. The approach is also traced to Harvard Business School teaching in the 1960s. Treat the attribution as "commonly credited".
  • Introduction, slide 7: the grading list jumps from item 3 to item 5 (there is no item 4). Items 1 to 3 add up to 10 + 3 + 7 = 20 points, separate from the exam out of 20.
