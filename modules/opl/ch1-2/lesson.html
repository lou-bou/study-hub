Linear Programming Primer

From a word problem to canonical form, standard form and the graphical method, with every example worked step by step. (Programmation linéaire)

The whole chapter in one minute. You can choose a few things (for example, how many of each product to make). You want one total to be as big as possible (profit) or as small as possible (cost). But you have limits (materials, time). If every formula is a straight line, that is a linear program. This page teaches you to (1) write such a problem as formulas, (2) rewrite the formulas in standard shapes, and (3) see the answer on a drawing.

What is a linear program? (programme linéaire)

↑ contents
In plain words

Choose some numbers to make one quantity as large (or small) as possible, while respecting limits. The catch that makes it "linear": every formula involved is a straight-line formula, no products or squares of variables.

Everyday picture. You run a small workshop. You get to choose how many of each product to make (the decision variables). You want the biggest possible profit (the objective function). But materials and time are limited (the constraints).
Why "linear"? Because everything scales in a straight line: make twice as many, earn twice the profit, use twice the material. No discounts, no bonuses, no products of unknowns.
  • Mathematical program (programme mathématique)Find the extremum (max or min) of a function of several variables, where the variables must satisfy a system of equations and/or inequalities.
  • Linear program, LP (programme linéaire, PL)A mathematical program where every function involved is linear.
  • Linear optimization (optimisation linéaire, OL)Finding the optimum of a linear program.
  • Decision variables (variables de décision)The unknowns x1…xn you get to choose.
  • Objective function (fonction économique, objectif)The linear quantity z you maximise or minimise.
  • Constraints (contraintes)Linear equations or inequalities the variables must obey.
Worked example · the first max problem, in full detail

The problem. max z = 3x + 2y subject to x + y ≤ 4, x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0.

A story that matches it. You have 4 hours to split between two jobs. Job X pays 3 per hour and job Y pays 2 per hour. Let x = hours spent on X and y = hours spent on Y. How should you split your time to earn the most?

  1. Read each piece.
    • z = 3x + 2y is the objective function: your total pay (3 per hour of X plus 2 per hour of Y).
    • x + y ≤ 4 is the constraint: hours used cannot exceed the 4 you have. It says "≤", not "=", because you are allowed to leave some hours unused.
    • x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0 are the sign constraints: you can't work a negative number of hours.
  2. Try some plans. A plan is a pair (x, y). It is feasible (réalisable) only if it respects every constraint. Only feasible plans are allowed to compete.
    Plan (x, y)x + yHours ≤ 4?x, y ≥ 0?Feasible?z = 3x + 2y
    (1, 1)2yesyesyes5
    (2, 2)4yesyesyes10
    (3, 1)4yesyesyes11
    (0, 4)4yesyesyes8
    (4, 0)4yesyesyes12
    (3, 3)6noyesno(15, but not allowed)
    (−1, 5)4yesnono(7, but not allowed)
    The plan (3, 3) looks great on paper (z = 15) but needs 6 hours, so it is out. The best feasible plan so far is (4, 0). But are we sure nothing beats it? That is what the next steps settle.
  3. Draw all feasible plans. Every plan (x, y) is a point on a plane. The edge x + y = 4 is a straight line, and "≤" keeps the side containing the origin. The two sign constraints keep the upper-right quarter. What is left is a triangle with corners (0,0), (4,0) and (0,4). Every feasible plan lies in this triangle, and this triangle is the domain.
  4. Why only the corners matter. All plans with the same pay lie on one straight line (for example 3x + 2y = 6, the dashed line). Pushing that line up and to the right raises the pay. The last point of the triangle it touches is where pay is highest, and because a triangle has flat sides, that last point is always a corner (or, in a special case, a whole side). So it is enough to check the corners.
  5. Compute z at each corner.
    CornerCalculationz
    (0, 0)3·0 + 2·00
    (4, 0)3·4 + 2·012
    (0, 4)3·0 + 2·48
  6. Answer. The optimum is x = 4, y = 0, z = 12: all 4 hours go to job X.
  7. Does it make sense in plain words? Yes. The only limit is the total number of hours, and job X pays more per hour (3 against 2), so every hour should go to X.
  8. What if the numbers change? Suppose job Y paid 5 per hour: z = 3x + 5y. Now the corners give 0, 12 and 20, so the best corner becomes (0, 4) with z = 20. The best corner depends on the objective, which is exactly why we compare corners instead of guessing.

For contrast, max z = xy is not linear: the objective multiplies two variables, so doubling both would quadruple z.

Modelling (modélisation)

↑ contents
In plain words

Turning a story into an LP is always the same four steps: name the unknowns, write the objective, write one constraint per limited resource, add x ≥ 0.

Problem 1: two products, three raw materials

Picture a small workshop. It makes two products, A and B, from three raw materials, M1, M2, M3. The stock is limited: 8 units of M1, 7 of M2, 3 of M3. Each unit of A earns 4 and each unit of B earns 5. Making one unit of A eats 2 units of M1 and 1 of M2 (no M3). Making one unit of B eats 1 unit of M1, 2 of M2 and 1 of M3. How many of each should it make?

Where the table comes from: it is just the story above written compactly. Each row is a raw material, each column is a product, and the number where they cross is "how much of that material one unit of that product uses". The last column is the stock.

ABStock
M1218
M2127
M3013
How to build the model, step by step.
  1. Unknowns. What do we choose? How many A and how many B. Call them xA and xB.
  2. Objective. Each A earns 4 and each B earns 5, so the total profit is 4xA + 5xB. (Example: 3 A and 2 B give 4·3 + 5·2 = 22.)
  3. One constraint per material: read one row of the table. M1 row: each A uses 2, each B uses 1, so the total M1 used is 2xA + 1xB, and it cannot exceed the stock of 8. The M2 row gives xA + 2xB ≤ 7. In the M3 row, A uses 0, so only xB ≤ 3 is left.
  4. No negative quantities. You can't make −2 units, so xA, xB ≥ 0.
Quick check with 3 A and 2 B: M1 used 2·3 + 2 = 8 ≤ 8, M2 used 3 + 2·2 = 7 ≤ 7, M3 used 2 ≤ 3. All limits hold, so this plan is feasible (admissible).
max z = 4x_A + 5x_B 2x_A + x_B ≤ 8 (M1) x_A + 2x_B ≤ 7 (M2) x_B ≤ 3 (M3) x_A, x_B ≥ 0
Worked example · Problem 1 solved, step by step
  1. Draw each limit as a line. Replace "≤" by "=". M1: 2xA + xB = 8 crosses the axes at (4, 0) and (0, 8). M2: xA + 2xB = 7 crosses at (7, 0) and (0, 3.5). M3: xB = 3 is a horizontal line. The origin satisfies all three, so the domain is on the origin side of each line (this is the polygon in the figure at the top).
  2. Find the corners. A corner is where two boundary lines meet and the point still respects every other limit. Checking the meeting points:
    Lines that meetPointRespects all limits?z = 4xA + 5xB
    xA = 0 and xB = 0(0, 0)yes0

  5. Meaning. Optimum: xA = 3, xB = 2, z = 22. M1 and M2 are used up completely (6 + 2 = 8 and 3 + 4 = 7): they are the bottlenecks. M3 has 1 unit left over (2 used out of 3).

Problem 2: production programme

A workshop has two machines, M1 (6000 min/month available) and M2 (4000 min/month). Product P1 needs 30 min on M1 and 40 min on M2 and earns 400. Product P2 needs 20 min on M1 and 10 min on M2 and earns 200. (Here M1 and M2 are machines, not the materials of Problem 1.)

Same recipe: the unknowns are how many P1 and P2 to make (x1, x2). The "resource" is machine time. Machine M1 runs 30x1 + 20x2 minutes and only has 6000, which gives the first constraint. The same reasoning on M2 gives the second.

max z = 400x_1 + 200x_2 30x_1 + 20x_2 ≤ 6000 (M1) 40x_1 + 10x_2 ≤ 4000 (M2) x_1, x_2 ≥ 0
Worked example · Problem 2 solved, step by step
  1. Test two plans to see what "feasible" means. Plan (50, 200): M1 uses 30·50 + 20·200 = 5500 ≤ 6000 and M2 uses 40·50 + 10·200 = 4000 ≤ 4000, so it is allowed, and it earns 400·50 + 200·200 = 60 000. Plan (60, 250) would earn 74 000, which looks better, but M1 would need 30·60 + 20·250 = 6800 > 6000, so it is not allowed.
  2. Try one product only. Only P2: M1 allows 6000 ÷ 20 = 300 units and M2 allows 4000 ÷ 10 = 400, so the limit is 300, earning 200·300 = 60 000. Only P1: M1 allows 200 and M2 allows 100, so the limit is 100, earning 40 000. Mixing both must do at least as well. Let us find out how much better.
  3. Find the corners of the domain. The M1 line crosses the axes at (200, 0) and (0, 300); the M2 line at (100, 0) and (0, 400). Only some of these are corners: (0, 300) and (100, 0) respect both limits; (200, 0) breaks M2 (8000 > 4000) and (0, 400) breaks M1 (8000 > 6000). The two lines meet at (40, 240).
  4. How (40, 240) was found. Multiply the M2 equation by 2: 80x1 + 20x2 = 8000. Subtract the M1 equation: 50x1 = 2000, so x1 = 40. Then 20x2 = 6000 − 1200 = 4800, so x2 = 240.
  5. Compare the corners.
    Corner(0, 0)(100, 0)(40, 240)(0, 300)
    z = 400x1 + 200x2040 00064 00060 000
  6. Meaning. Optimum: x1 = 40, x2 = 240, z = 64 000. Both machines are saturated: 1200 + 4800 = 6000 and 1600 + 2400 = 4000. The mix beats the best single product (60 000). Section 9 solves this same problem by drawing.

Problem 3: transport problem (problème de transport)

Ship goods from n depots to m shops. Known: unit cost cij, depot stock ai, shop demand bj. Unknown: quantity xij sent from depot i to shop j.

Reading the notation. Two small numbers under a letter are just an address: x12 = amount sent from depot 1 to shop 2, and c12 = the cost of sending one unit along that route. The symbol Σ means "add up over all values". So the objective reads "cost per unit × units sent, added over every route". The two constraints say, in words: each depot ships out exactly what it has (add up one row of shipments) and each shop receives exactly what it needs (add up one column).
min Z = Σᵢ Σⱼ c_ij x_ij Σⱼ x_ij = a_i (each depot ships its whole stock) Σᵢ x_ij = b_j (each shop receives exactly its demand) x_ij ≥ 0

Canonical form (forme canonique)

↑ contents
In plain words

A standard "shape" that every LP can be forced into: maximise, all constraints are "≤", all variables are "≥ 0". Having one shape means one method can handle everything.

max z = Σⱼ c_j x_j (objective) Σⱼ a_ij x_j ≤ b_i i = 1…m (proper constraints) x_j ≥ 0 j = 1…n (sign constraints)
  • Proper constraints (contraintes propres)The inequalities bounding linear expressions by constants.
  • Sign constraints (contraintes impropres)The conditions xj ≥ 0.
  • Feasible (admissible) solution (solution admissible)A point satisfying every constraint.
  • Optimal solution (solution optimale)A feasible point where the objective reaches its maximum.
  • Domain (domaine)The set of all feasible solutions.

Conversion rules

You haveReplace by
min zmax(−z); the optimal value of the min is minus the optimal value of the max
Σ aijxj ≥ biΣ (−aij)xj ≤ −bi
Σ aijxj = biboth ≤ bi and ≥ bi
xj ≤ 0substitute xj = −x'j with x'j ≥ 0
xj free (any real)xj = x'j − x''j with both ≥ 0
Why each rule is allowed.
  • min → max(−z). The smallest number is the one whose negative is the largest. Example: the numbers 3, 5, 8 have minimum 3, and −3, −5, −8 have maximum −3. The same point wins; only the sign of the value flips.
  • "≥" → "≤". Multiplying both sides by −1 flips the direction: 5 ≥ 3 becomes −5 ≤ −3.
  • "=" → two inequalities. "a equals b" means "a ≤ b and a ≥ b" at the same time.
  • x ≤ 0. Just rename: if x = −4, then x′ = 4 is positive. Work with x′ and switch back at the end.
  • Free variable. Any real number is a difference of two non-negative numbers: 5 = 5 − 0 and −7 = 0 − 7. So x = x′ − x″.
Worked example · min and "≥"

min z = 3x1 + 2x2, x1 + x2 ≥ 4, x1 ≤ 3, x ≥ 0.

  1. Look at the objective. It is a "min", so replace it by max(−z) = −3x1 − 2x2.
  2. Look at each constraint. x1 + x2 ≥ 4 has the wrong direction: multiply by −1 to get −x1 − x2 ≤ −4. x1 ≤ 3 is already "≤", so keep it. The sign conditions are already fine.
  3. Result.
    max (−z) = −3x_1 − 2x_2 −x_1 − x_2 ≤ −4 x_1 ≤ 3 x_1, x_2 ≥ 0
  4. Check that the answer is the same. In words: you need a total of at least 4 units, and x2 costs 2 per unit against 3 for x1, so use only x2: the plan (0, 4) costs 8. The plan (3, 1) costs 11, worse. In the canonical form the best value of −z is −8 at the same point, and min z = −(−8) = 8.
Worked example · an equality

max z = 3x1 + 2x2, x1 + x2 = 5, x1 − x2 ≥ −1, x ≥ 0.

  1. The "=" constraint becomes two: x1 + x2 ≤ 5 and x1 + x2 ≥ 5, which after multiplying by −1 is −x1 − x2 ≤ −5.
  2. The "≥" constraint: x1 − x2 ≥ −1 becomes −x1 + x2 ≤ 1.
  3. Result.
    max 3x_1 + 2x_2 x_1 + x_2 ≤ 5 −x_1 − x_2 ≤ −5 −x_1 + x_2 ≤ 1 x_1, x_2 ≥ 0
  4. Check. The total is fixed at 5 and x1 pays more (3 against 2), so take everything as x1: (5, 0), z = 15. Original constraints hold: 5 + 0 = 5, and 5 − 0 = 5 ≥ −1.
Worked example · a variable ≤ 0

max z = x1 − 2x2, x1 − x2 ≤ 4, x1 ≥ 0, x2 ≤ 0.

  1. Rename. Put x2 = −y with y ≥ 0.
  2. Substitute everywhere. The objective x1 − 2x2 becomes x1 + 2y. The constraint x1 − x2 ≤ 4 becomes x1 + y ≤ 4.
  3. Result.
    max x_1 + 2y x_1 + y ≤ 4 x_1, y ≥ 0
  4. Solve and translate back. y pays 2 per unit against 1 for x1, so take y = 4, x1 = 0: z = 8. Going back, x2 = −y = −4. Check in the original: 0 − (−4) = 4 ≤ 4 and z = 0 − 2·(−4) = 8.
Worked example · a free variable

max z = x1 + 3x2, x1 + 2x2 ≤ 4, x2 ≤ 3, x2 ≥ 0, with x1 free (it may be negative).

  1. Split the free variable. Write x1 = u − v with u, v ≥ 0.
  2. Substitute everywhere.
    max u − v + 3x_2 u − v + 2x_2 ≤ 4 x_2 ≤ 3 u, v, x_2 ≥ 0
  3. Solve and translate back. The best is u = 0, v = 2, x2 = 3, so x1 = u − v = −2. Check: −2 + 2·3 = 4 ≤ 4 and z = −2 + 9 = 7.
  4. Why the freedom mattered. If x1 had to be ≥ 0, the best plan would be (0, 2) with only z = 6. Also note that u and v are not unique: (u, v) = (1, 3) also gives x1 = −2. That is harmless; only the difference counts.

Geometric view (interprétation géométrique)

↑ contents
In plain words

With two variables, every solution is a point in the plane. Each constraint cuts the plane with a line and keeps one side. What remains is a polygon, and the best point is a corner of it.

  • Half-plane (demi-plan)The set of points satisfying one inequality; its border is the line obtained by turning "≤" into "=".
  • DomainThe intersection of all the half-planes (3 proper + 2 sign constraints = 5 half-planes in the example below).
  • Vertex (sommet)A corner of the polygon.

Method

Draw the lines z = constant (parallel lines, all with the same slope). Slide them in the direction that increases z. The last line still touching the domain gives the optimal solutions.

How to draw one constraint. Turn the "≤" into "=": that gives a straight line (find two points on it, for example where it crosses each axis). The constraint keeps one side of that line, the half-plane. To know which side, test the point (0,0): if it satisfies the inequality, keep the side with the origin, otherwise keep the other side. Do this for every constraint; what is left after all of them is the domain.
Why the optimum is at a corner. All points with the same z lie on one straight line. Sliding that line changes z steadily. The domain has flat sides, so the last point the line touches is a corner (or a whole flat side if the line is parallel to it). That is why checking the corners is enough.
The full method on a real problem is worked out in section 9.

  2. Pick the side of each line. Test the origin: 0 ≤ 1, 0 ≤ 10 and 0 ≤ 4 are all true, so keep the origin side of all three.
  3. Find the corners. Check where lines meet and whether the point respects everything else:

Optimum not unique

The optimum is usually one vertex, but not always: if the objective lines are parallel to an edge, the whole edge is optimal.

Worked example, step by step

max z = x1 + x2, x1 + x2 ≤ 4, x1 ≤ 3, x2 ≤ 3, x ≥ 0.

  1. Corners. (0,0), (3,0), (3,1), (1,3), (0,3). The point (3,1) is where x1 = 3 meets x1 + x2 = 4; (1,3) is where x2 = 3 meets it.
  2. Values of z. (0,0) → 0, (3,0) → 3, (0,3) → 3, (3,1) → 4, (1,3) → 4. Two corners tie at 4.
  3. Why the tie. The objective lines are x1 + x2 = constant, which have exactly the same direction as the edge x1 + x2 = 4. When the sliding line reaches the value 4, it lies along that edge instead of touching a single point.
  4. Consequence. Every point on the edge between (1,3) and (3,1) is feasible and gives z = 4, for example (2,2): 2 + 2 = 4. So there are infinitely many optimal plans, and you can choose among them using any other criterion.

Every point from (1,3) to (3,1) gives z = 4, which is the optimum.

The three assumptions (hypothèses)

↑ contents
In plain words

A real situation can only be modelled as an LP if it behaves in a "straight-line" way. Check these three before trusting the model.

  • 1. Independence / additivity (additivité)Activities don't influence each other, so totals are sums. This is what allows sums in the objective and in the constraints. Simple test: the profit of 3 A plus 2 B is just 3 × (profit of A) + 2 × (profit of B), with no bonus or penalty for combining them.
  • 2. Proportionality (proportionnalité)Gain and resource use scale directly with the activity level. This keeps the formulas linear. Simple test: double the quantity, and the profit and the materials used both double.
  • 3. Divisibility (divisibilité)Variables may take fractional values (you can make 2.5 units). If not, you have an integer linear program (programmation linéaire en nombres entiers), much harder to solve.
Example · additivity fails

Suppose A earns 4, B earns 5, but making both earns an extra bonus of 3. Then one A and one B earn 4 + 5 + 3 = 12 instead of 4 + 5 = 9. The profit now depends on the combination, so it is no longer a sum of separate pieces. It would need a term like xAxB, which is not linear.

Example · proportionality fails

A supplier charges 10 per unit for the first 100 units and 8 for the next ones. 100 units cost 1000, but 200 units cost 1000 + 800 = 1800, not 2000. Doubling the quantity did not double the cost, so cost is not a straight line in the quantity.

Example · divisibility

max x1 + x2, 2x1 + 2x2 ≤ 5.

  1. Simplify. Dividing the constraint by 2: x1 + x2 ≤ 2.5.
  2. With fractions allowed, z can reach 2.5, for example x1 = 2.5, x2 = 0.
  3. With whole numbers only, x1 + x2 must be an integer ≤ 2.5, so the best is 2.
  4. Lesson. The two answers differ (2.5 against 2), so the integer problem is a different, harder problem. In general you cannot simply round the LP answer: the rounded point may break a constraint or not be the best whole-number plan.

Standard form (forme standard)

↑ contents
In plain words

Instead of inequalities, use only equalities (apart from x ≥ 0). To turn an inequality into an equality you add a non-negative "leftover" variable.

Why add a variable? Take the M1 constraint of Problem 1: 2xA + xB ≤ 8. There can be a gap between what you use and what you have. Call that gap s1 (the unused M1). Then 2xA + xB + s1 = 8 is an exact equality, and s1 ≥ 0 because you can't use more than you have. Equalities matter because the solving method you will see later (the simplex method) works by solving systems of equations.
  • Standard form (forme standard)All constraints, except xj ≥ 0, are equalities.
  • Slack variable (variable d'écart)A new variable ≥ 0 measuring the gap in an inequality. For "≤" you add it; for "≥" you subtract it. It has coefficient 0 in the objective.
Course example, redone step by step

(P): max f = 5x1 − 3x2, 2x1 + 3x2 ≥ 4, x1 − x2 ≤ 2, −x1 + 6x2 = 10, x ≥ 0.

  1. Go through the constraints one at a time.
    • 2x1 + 3x2 ≥ 4: the left side may be bigger than 4, so subtract the extra: 2x1 + 3x2 − x3 = 4 with x3 ≥ 0.
    • x1 − x2 ≤ 2: the left side may be smaller than 2, so add the missing part: x1 − x2 + x4 = 2 with x4 ≥ 0.
    • −x1 + 6x2 = 10 is already an equality: leave it.
  2. Objective. The new variables get coefficient 0 because they don't earn anything.
  3. Result.
    (P') max f = 5x_1 − 3x_2 + 0x_3 + 0x_4 2x_1 + 3x_2 − x_3 = 4 (≥ : subtract x_3) x_1 − x_2 + x_4 = 2 (≤ : add x_4) −x_1 + 6x_2 = 10 (already an equality) x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4 ≥ 0
  4. Find the optimum. The solution makes two constraints "tight" (exactly met): x1 − x2 = 2 and −x1 + 6x2 = 10. Add them: 5x2 = 12, so x2 = 2.4 and x1 = 4.4. Then f = 5·4.4 − 3·2.4 = 22 − 7.2 = 14.8.
  5. Read the extra variables. x4 = 2 − (4.4 − 2.4) = 0: the "≤" constraint is tight, no gap. x3 = 2·4.4 + 3·2.4 − 4 = 16 − 4 = 12: the "≥" constraint is satisfied with plenty of room. (P) and (P') have the same optimum.
Problem 1 in standard form
max z = 4x_A + 5x_B 2x_A + x_B + s_1 = 8 x_A + 2x_B + s_2 = 7 x_B + s_3 = 3 x_A, x_B, s_1, s_2, s_3 ≥ 0

One slack per "≤" constraint, so three slacks. At the optimum (xA, xB) = (3, 2), each slack is simply "stock minus what was used":

MaterialUsedStockSlack = stock − used
M12·3 + 2 = 88s1 = 0
M23 + 2·2 = 77s2 = 0
M323s3 = 1

So s = (0, 0, 1): M1 and M2 fully used, 1 unit of M3 unused.

8

Matrix form (écriture matricielle)

↑ contents
In plain words

The standard form written compactly with a profit vector, a matrix of coefficients and a right-hand-side vector.

How to read it. A is just the table of coefficients of the equations. Each row is one constraint and each column is one variable. Ax = b means "row 1 times the variables equals the first right-hand side, row 2 the second, and so on". ctx means "profit of each variable times its value, added up". It is the same model as before, only shorter to write.
max f = cᵗx Ax = b x ≥ 0
  • ct (vecteur profit)Vector in ℝⁿ of the objective coefficients.
  • A (matrice des contraintes)m × n matrix of the aij; assumed to have rank m.
  • bRight-hand-side vector in ℝᵐ.
  • Feasible solution (solution réalisable)Any x with Ax = b and x ≥ 0.
  • Optimal solution (solution optimale)A feasible x* with f(x) ≤ f(x*) for every feasible x.
Problem 1, redone in matrix form
  1. List the variables in a fixed order: the 2 decisions then the 3 slacks, so x = (xA, xB, s1, s2, s3)t.
  2. Copy the coefficients of each equation of the standard form into a row, in that order. A missing variable gets 0.
    x = (x_A, x_B, s_1, s_2, s_3)ᵗ c = (4, 5, 0, 0, 0)ᵗ b = (8, 7, 3)ᵗ ⎡ 2 1 1 0 0 ⎤ A = ⎢ 1 2 0 1 0 ⎥ ⎣ 0 1 0 0 1 ⎦
  3. Size and rank. A has 3 rows (constraints) and 5 columns (variables). Its last three columns form the identity matrix, so its rank is 3 = m, as the course assumes.
  4. Check the optimum row by row. With x* = (3, 2, 0, 0, 1):
    • row 1: 2·3 + 1·2 + 1·0 + 0 + 0 = 8 = b1
    • row 2: 1·3 + 2·2 + 0 + 1·0 + 0 = 7 = b2
    • row 3: 0 + 1·2 + 0 + 0 + 1·1 = 3 = b3
    So Ax* = b and all entries are ≥ 0: x* is feasible.
  5. Value. ctx* = 4·3 + 5·2 + 0 + 0 + 0 = 22.
9

Graphical resolution (résolution graphique)

↑ contents
In plain words

With two unknowns you can solve an LP by drawing it. Draw the region of allowed plans, then slide a line of constant profit across it. The last point it touches is the best plan.

  • Production programme (programme de production)How many units of each product to make per month.
  • Domain D of feasible solutions (domaine des solutions réalisables)The region of all plans that respect every constraint.
  • Vertices, extreme points (sommets, points extrêmes)The corners of D.
  • Convex polytope (polytope convexe)What the course calls D in general. "Convex" means no dents: the straight segment joining any two points of D stays inside D. When D is bounded (its points stay at a finite distance from the origin), the course calls it a convex polyhedron (polyèdre convexe).
  • Gradient ∇z (gradient)The vector of the objective's coefficients. It points in the direction in which z increases fastest.
  • Optimal solution x* (solution optimale)The best feasible plan; z* is its value.
Course example, step by step · the workshop problem

A mechanical workshop has two machines M1 and M2 and makes two products P1 and P2. The table gives, per product, the working time on each machine and the profit per unit, and, per machine, the time available per month (UM = monetary units):

P1P2Time available
M130 min20 min6000 min/month
M240 min10 min4000 min/month
Unit profit400 UM200 UM

This is exactly Problem 2 of section 2. The goal is to find the production programme (units of each product per month) that gives the maximum profit, given the machine time.

  1. Name the unknowns (the decision variables): x1 and x2, the monthly quantities of P1 and P2.
  2. Write the constraints of the problem (the conditions for the production to be possible): 30x1 + 20x2 ≤ 6000 for M1, 40x1 + 10x2 ≤ 4000 for M2, and x1 ≥ 0, x2 ≥ 0.
  3. Write the objective function (also called the economic function): the owner wants maximum profit, z = 400x1 + 200x2.
  4. Put it together: this is the linear program (PL).
    (PL) Max z = 400x_1 + 200x_2 30x_1 + 20x_2 ≤ 6000 40x_1 + 10x_2 ≤ 4000 x_1 ≥ 0, x_2 ≥ 0
    The best solution, called the optimal solution x* = (x1*, x2*)t, tells you the quantities to make to get the maximum profit z* = 400x1* + 200x2*.
  5. Draw the two lines. 30x1 + 20x2 = 6000 crosses the axes at (200, 0) and (0, 300). 40x1 + 10x2 = 4000 crosses at (100, 0) and (0, 400). The origin satisfies both inequalities (0 ≤ 6000 and 0 ≤ 4000), so keep the origin side of both.
  6. Read the domain D. The points that satisfy all constraints form the quadrilateral OABC (the shaded area). Its corners O, A, B, C are the vertices (extreme points) of D.
  7. Find the coordinates of the vertices.
    • O = (0, 0): the two axes meet.
    • A = (100, 0): the M2 line meets the x1-axis (40x1 = 4000). Check M1: 30·100 = 3000 ≤ 6000 ✓.
    • C = (0, 300): the M1 line meets the x2-axis (20x2 = 6000). Check M2: 10·300 = 3000 ≤ 4000 ✓.
    • B = (40, 240): the two lines meet. Multiply the M2 equation by 2 (80x1 + 20x2 = 8000) and subtract the M1 equation: 50x1 = 2000, so x1 = 40, then 20x2 = 6000 − 1200, so x2 = 240.
    The other axis crossings, (200, 0) and (0, 400), are not vertices: (200, 0) breaks M2 (8000 > 4000) and (0, 400) breaks M1 (8000 > 6000).
  8. Understand the gradient. The gradient of z is the vector of its coefficients, ∇z = (400, 200). In plain words: one more unit of P1 adds 400, one more unit of P2 adds 200, so profit grows twice as fast along x1 as along x2, which is the direction (2, 1) of the orange arrow. The lines "z = constant" are perpendicular to this arrow. The course states that z increases when you move perpendicularly to those lines in the same direction as the gradient.
  9. Slide the line. Start with a profit line such as 400x1 + 200x2 = 20 000 (dashed), and push it in the direction of the gradient. The last vertex of D that it meets gives an optimal solution: here that is B.
  10. Cross-check with the corner values.
    VertexO (0, 0)A (100, 0)B (40, 240)C (0, 300)
    z = 400x1 + 200x2040 00064 00060 000
  11. Answer. There is a unique optimal solution, the point B: x1* = 40, x2* = 240, z* = 64 000. In words: make 40 units of P1 and 240 units of P2 per month for a monthly profit of 64 000 UM, using both machines to the full.

When the last vertex is not unique

If the line of constant profit ends up lying along an edge of D instead of touching a single corner, every point of that edge is optimal. There are then infinitely many optimal solutions. The course calls this case dégénéré (see the notes at the end for the usual vocabulary).

Worked example · the workshop with a different profit

Keep the same constraints but suppose P1 earns 300 instead of 400: z = 300x1 + 200x2.

  1. Spot the parallel. Notice that 300x1 + 200x2 = 10 · (30x1 + 20x2). So the profit lines have exactly the direction of the M1 line, that is, of the edge CB.
  2. Read z on that edge. Every point of the edge CB satisfies 30x1 + 20x2 = 6000, so there z = 10 × 6000 = 60 000. Check: C (0, 300) → 60 000, B (40, 240) → 12 000 + 48 000 = 60 000, and the midpoint (20, 270) → 6000 + 54 000 = 60 000. The vertex A gives only 30 000.
  3. Answer. Every point of the edge from C to B is optimal, with z* = 60 000. The optimum is not unique.
Notes: errors and loose wording found in the original course
  • Sign error in "min z = max(−z)". The first PDF writes it this way; it is only the solution that is shared. The optimal value flips sign: min z = −max(−z) (the second PDF states it correctly). In section 3's example, min z = 8 while max(−z) = −8.
  • "Slack" vs "surplus". In the standard-form example both x3 and x4 are called variables d'écart, but x3 is subtracted (a "≥" constraint). Strictly, that one is a surplus variable (variable d'excédent); only x4 is a slack.
  • Transport problem needs a balance condition. With equalities on both sides, the problem has a solution only if total stock equals total demand (Σai = Σbj). The course never says so.
  • Polytope vs polyhedron. The graphical-resolution page says D is a "polytope convexe" in general and a "polyèdre convexe" when bounded. The usual convention is the other way round: a polyhedron is any intersection of half-planes (bounded or not), and a polytope is a bounded polyhedron. Use the course's wording in exams, but know the usual one.
  • "Cas dégénéré". The course uses "dégénéré" for the case of infinitely many optimal solutions (a whole edge). The usual name for that is multiple or alternative optima. In the simplex method "degenerate" normally means something else (a vertex where more constraints are tight than necessary). Use the course's word in exams.
  • Typos. "Maxixmiser" in the Problem 1 title; "A est le rang m" should read "A est de rang m".
