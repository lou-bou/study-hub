1

Project, sponsor, project manager (projet, mandant, chef de projet)

↑ contents
In plain words

A project is a one-off, somewhat new piece of work that someone asked for, with a goal, a deadline and a budget. One person orders it and answers for the result (the sponsor). Another person runs it day to day (the project manager).

  • project (projet)A set of complex actions meant to satisfy a sponsor's need.
  • sponsor / client (mandant, donneur d'ordre, commanditaire)Answers for results, cost and deadline. Always one person from the permanent organisation. Starts and steers the project, provides resources, names the project manager and the steering committee (comité de pilotage).
  • project manager (chef de projet)Supervises execution from A to Z: assigns people, tracks schedule and budget, links client and team.
  • funder (sponsor)An outside party that pays for part of the project. The same person can sometimes be sponsor and manager.
Worked example · USTHB research projects (PRFU)

Check a PRFU (Projet de Recherche et Formation Universitaire) against the 7 conditions.

  • Sponsor: the Ministry of Higher Education.
  • Project manager: a senior lecturer-researcher (Professor or Maître de Conférences) attached to a research lab.
  • Unique and new: each research topic is new by nature.
  • Goal, deadline, budget: fixed when the project is approved.
  • 7 / 7 conditions met, so a PRFU is a project.

Project or not? (projet ou non-projet)

↑ contents
In plain words

Repetitive work and tiny jobs are not projects. A mission is a project only if every condition holds. If even one red flag appears (routine, no end date, very small amount), it is not. Calling everything a project clogs the project system.

  • routine task (tâche répétitive, travail de routine)Recurring work with no end. Never a project.
  • minimum threshold (coût minimal)Many companies set a minimum amount below which work is not a project.in Switzerland, thresholds such as 50'000 or 250'000 CHF; other countries have none.
  • checklist (liste de contrôle)The 7 conditions of section 1, ticked one by one.
Worked example · the course's six missions
MissionVerdictWhy
By end of December, raise sales of product X in South-East Asia by 15% (value and volume)projectclear goal, end date, measurable
In 8 months, cut returns for manufacturing defects by 20%projectgoal + deadline
Before year end, replace all local printers with networked department printersprojectone-off, dated

Keep department printers availablenotroutine

Note the pair "replace the printers" (project) and "keep the printers available" (routine): same topic, different nature.

3

Starting a project (débuter un projet)

↑ contents
In plain words

Ideally five things exist at the start: someone who pays and benefits, a sponsor, a manager, a problem or need, and a goal. Often the goal is missing; defining it with the sponsor is then the manager's first job. Then you plan milestones where the sponsor gives the green light.

  • client (client)Pays for the project and benefits from it.
  • milestone (jalon)A significant event with a date that can be known for sure. Reached only when its result is irreversibly acquired (a delivery, an end of phase).
  • project management plan (plan de management de projet)The plan agreed with the sponsor, if the project size calls for one.
  • acceptance (recette)The final reception of the project: the last milestone.
The manager's first five steps
  1. Rephrase the problem or need in your own words and have the sponsor check it.
  2. Make sure the goals and expected deliverables are understood.
  3. Agree the main phases: pre-project? formal project request? management plan?
  4. Start from an existing list of project activities and strike out what is not needed.
  5. Plan the milestones where you want the sponsor's green light.
Worked example · the "Web page" project that starts without a goal
  • Everyone agrees the company "needs a web page". Client? the company. Need? vaguely "be visible".
  • Startup checklist: sponsor defined? client identified? need understood? goal formulated? Only the client is clear.
  • First job of the manager: hold talks with the sponsor until the goal is written down, for example "a 5-page site presenting our products, online before the trade fair".
  • First milestone = "goals agreed". Trap: a project everyone agrees on but that still has no sponsor.
4

Finding the real need (identifier les problèmes ou les besoins)

↑ contents
In plain words

A project starts from a problem (something that hurts) or a need (something wanted). Before building anything, find out what users really want. The single biggest success factor: spend time with the end users, not only their bosses. Keep asking "why?" to reach the real problem behind the stated one.

  • problem (problème)Users are unhappy with something existing.a badly designed car dashboard.
  • need (besoin)Users want something new.a GPS in the car.
  • 5 whys method (méthode des cinq pourquoi)Ask "why?" repeatedly to separate the false problem from the true one.
  • stakeholders (parties prenantes)Everyone concerned. Interview all categories, with open questions.
Worked example · the purchasing department's lack of space
  1. Why new offices? Not enough room: three people per office.
  2. Why three per office? Computers waiting to be checked fill the whole floor.
  3. Why check them here? The supplier used to do the checks; now we must.
  4. Why did the supplier stop? (asked to the head of purchasing) The supplier wanted 70% more for the checks.
  5. Why the price rise? (asked to the supplier) Our QA manager's demands cost 4 hours per computer.

Fix: soften the QA requirements. The supplier resumes the checks, the floor empties, no new offices needed. To feel the scale: at, say, 50 computers a month, 4 h each is 200 hours of checks a month (illustrative volume, not from the course).

The course's three field cases make the same point: insurance agents followed for 2 to 3 days before designing their laptops, a team spending a day at the Algiers marshalling yard (gare de triage), and a CEO visiting 28 clients in 6 weeks (about 4.7 a week) before launching three successful machines.

5

Choosing the project manager (choisir le chef de projet)

↑ contents
In plain words

Pick someone who has led a similar project, or at least worked in one. They must communicate well, handle conflict, know the field and know project management. Technical skill alone is not enough, and neither is pure management without domain knowledge.

  • hands-on vs directing (mettre la main à la pâte / diriger)In small projects the manager also does the work; in large ones they only lead.
  • half-day rule (une demi-journée par collaborateur)A manager needs about half a day of organising per team member (per week).
  • coach (coach externe)An outside expert who supports a manager lacking one skill.
Worked example · FORMALUX's new production system (GPAO)
  • Context: a tool maker (one-off parts) whose BRIDOFLEX line became series production. Founder M. Crettaz wants a computer-aided production management system (GPAO) covering both.
  • Rule out: Gindraz, Queloz (technicians, little production knowledge), Franke, Laub (narrow view), Droz (all of production against him), Payot (good designer, poor manager).
  • Remaining: Meyer, production planning, wants the job, but struggles with "just-in-time".
  • Hiring an outsider would offend Meyer. Decision: Meyer + external coach.
  • Half-day rule check: with 10 full-time members, 10 × ½ day = 5 days a week, the whole week is spent on organising and leading.
6

Project organisation (organisation de projet)

↑ contents
In plain words

Every project gets a temporary structure that disappears at the end. In a matrix, members stay in their departments and have two bosses: flexible but conflict-prone. In a dedicated project organisation, the manager is everyone's boss: efficient, rigid, for long projects. A small purchase can do with a few coordination meetings.

  • matrix organisation (organisation matricielle)Members belong to their department and to the project; two bosses: line manager (chef de ligne) and project manager. Typical for small and medium projects, and for services.
  • project organisation (organisation de projet, structure divisionnelle par projet)The project manager is also the hierarchical boss. For large, long projects.
  • staff organisation (organisation en état-major)No real team; the manager coordinates through a few meetings.
  • project office (bureau de projet)A shared room where part-time members work on project days.
Advantages (avantages)Drawbacks (inconvénients)
Projecthigh autonomy of managers · strong team involvement · adapts to the marketcompany life tied to project length · little cross-communication · projects siloed
Matrixvalues the project function · motivates the team · balances workloaddouble hierarchy · managing seconded staff
Worked example · three cases, three structures
  • "HotLine 7/7" (insurer): 2 Marketing + 2 Field services + 1 App development + 1 HR + 1 Training + 2 Internal organisation = 9 members, + the manager = 10 people from 6 departments → matrix.
  • Buying 4 machining centres: interviews, weighting meeting, tender to 8–10 suppliers, choice meeting, approval. No team needed → staff organisation (état-major).
  • Motorway tunnel: 4 engineers + 2 draughtsmen full time for at least 3 years → project organisation.
  • Matrix staffing tip: 10 people at 10%, 5 at 20% and 2 at 50% all give 1.0 full-time equivalent. With the half-day rule, leading them costs 5, 2.5 and 1 day a week: prefer 2 people at 50%.
7

The pre-project (avant-projet)

↑ contents
In plain words

A pre-project is a small project whose results are studies, not products. It gathers the facts needed to decide whether and how to launch the real project. It costs resources, so a manager with spending authority must approve it. Do not confuse its goal with the project's goal, and do not hide risks.

  • pre-project (avant-projet, pré-étude, étude, pré-projet, étude de faisabilité)Same thing, many names.
  • typical outputsmarket study, profitability calculation (calcul de rentabilité), technical feasibility, risk analysis, delivery-time estimate, impact study.
  • pre-project request form (demande d'avant-projet)Situation, summary, goal of the pre-project, usefulness of the project, main milestones, planned resources.
Worked example · Mrs Jufer's HR software pre-project
  • Company: 1680 employees on 2 sites. Problems: staff data spread over 3 systems (costly updates, errors), appraisal data on paper only, unclear passwords and access rights.
  • Pre-project goal: get the data to specify the software, choose among market products, and estimate costs and benefits to ±20%.
  • Project goal (different!): replace the 3 systems with one, compatible with accounting, compliant with data-protection law, with clear access rules.
  • Phases: 15 Mar → 30 Apr = 46 days; 30 Apr → 20 May = 20 days; total pre-project 66 days; then 11 days to write the project request (31 May).
  • What ±20% means: if the estimate is 500 000 DA (illustrative), the true cost should fall between 400 000 and 600 000 DA.

Management process & life cycle (gestion de projet, cycle de vie)

↑ contents
In plain words

Project management means applying knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to reach the project's requirements. It follows the project from start to closure in five stages, each producing deliverables. A client review at the end of each stage decides whether to go on, so risk is cut into slices. Control runs across all stages.

  • initiate (initier)Analyse the project and decide go or no-go.
  • plan (planifier)Produces the project management plan (plan de management, business plan).
  • execute (exécuter)Carry out the work packages (lots de travaux) of the plan.
  • monitor & control (surveiller et maîtriser)Continuous, iterative, frequent, during execution.
  • close (clore)Wrap up with lessons learned, strengths and weaknesses, for the next similar project.
  • project life cycle (cycle de vie du projet)The sequence of these phases, with a client review between each.
Worked example · place each output of the HR software project
OutputPhase
Decision to go ahead after the pre-projectInitiation
Schedule of the migration from 3 systems to 1Planning
Weekly progress report of the teamExecution
Request from HR to add an appraisal module, accepted with a new dateControl (changes, deadlines)
Final review: what went well, what to do differentlyClosing (bilan)
9

The golden triangle (le triangle d'or, PCD)

↑ contents
In plain words

A project succeeds when it delivers what was promised, for the agreed cost, on the agreed date. Together these are called overall quality. Performance comes first, because if the technical work goes wrong, delays and overruns follow. Any new client request outside the contract must be renegotiated in money and in time.

  • performance (performance, qualité technique)The technical object of the project itself.
  • cost (coûts, qualité économique)Estimated item by item, overruns analysed and renegotiated.
  • deadline (délais, qualité temporelle)Delivery date: estimate durations, control slippage.
  • overall quality (qualité globale, PCD)The three together.
Worked example · a change request (illustrative numbers)
  • Contract: budget 1 200 000 DA, 6 months.
  • The client asks for an extra feature estimated at +15% of the work.
  • Cost rule: renegotiate financially → new budget 1 200 000 × 1.15 = 1 380 000 DA.
  • Deadline rule: carry it into the contractual calendar → about 6 × 1.15 ≈ 6.9 months, so roughly one extra month.
  • Accepting the feature "for free" breaks C and D, even if P is perfect.

Non-quality (la non-qualité)

↑ contents
In plain words

Missing any of P, C or D is non-quality, and fixing it costs more than doing it right first time. The later a problem is found, the more it costs. Over-quality is also wasteful. Aim for acceptable quality in between.

  • cost drift (dérive des coûts)Problem found early: add resources, keep the date, but blow the budget.
  • schedule and cost drift (dérive du planning et des coûts)Problem found late: even extra resources cannot save the date; extra cost + late penalties.
  • over-quality (surqualité)More than needed: costly and useless.

Stakeholders (parties prenantes : MOA et MOE)

↑ contents
In plain words

Every project has a client side that orders and pays, and a supplier side that does the work. The client side is the project owner (MOA); the supplier side is the prime contractor (MOE). The contract is the interface between them. Inside the supplier's company sit the project manager and the team.

  • project owner (maître d'ouvrage, MOA)The client, public or industrial, who awards the contracts. Its project director follows progress, attends reviews, controls finances, checks conformity to needs, informs its own client.
  • prime contractor (maître d'œuvre, MOE)The supplier, chosen for technical skill, who executes the work (or has it executed) within time, cost, resources and quality.
  • contract (contrat)The interface linking client and supplier.

Agile projects and Scrum (le projet agile)

↑ contents
In plain words

Classic life cycles plan everything upfront. Agile life cycles work in short loops, built on flexibility, collaboration, efficiency and speed. Scrum, the most used and well structured, cuts the work into sprints, and each sprint gives the client a usable deliverable. Kanban and SAFe are other agile methods.

  • agile life cycle (cycle de vie agile)Iterative, adaptive development instead of one long plan.
  • sprint (sprint, itération)A short fixed period (often 2 to 4 weeks) ending with a deliverable (livrable).
  • product backlog (carnet de produit)The ordered list of everything to build. Each sprint takes items from the top.
  • Scrum rolesProduct Owner (priorities), Scrum Master (process), development team.
  • agile tools (plateformes de gestion de projets agiles)Jira, ClickUp, Confluence: follow sprints end to end.
Worked example · how many sprints?
  • Backlog: 60 story points (an estimate of effort). The team completes about 12 points per sprint (its velocity).
  • Sprints needed: 60 / 12 = 5 sprints.
  • With 2-week sprints: 5 × 2 = 10 weeks, and the client receives 5 deliverables along the way instead of one at the end.
  • In Jira or ClickUp, each sprint is a board; the 5 sprints become the project's milestones.

Big Data jobs (les métiers du Big Data)

↑ contents
In plain words

A Big Data project needs three kinds of people: those who collect and store the data, those who process and analyse it, and those who protect it. Knowing who does what helps you staff the team (section 5) and organise it (section 6).

  • collection & storage (collecte et stockage)Data Engineer: builds collection systems, prepares data (SQL, Python). Chief Data Officer (CDO): links top management and the data team, leads it (Power BI, SQL, Python). Data Miner: explores and formats useful data. Big Data Architect: supervises retrieval, management, storage (NoSQL like Cassandra, Hadoop, Spark).
  • processing & analysis (traitement et analyse)Data Analyst: "makes the data talk" for managers (Python, SQL, data visualisation). Data Scientist: statistical and predictive models (Python, R, ML). Data Manager: availability, quality, GDPR compliance. ML Engineer: deep neural networks, models for images and text.
  • protection & security (protection et sécurisation)Data Protection Officer (DPO): security and legal compliance (GDPR, RGPD). Data Steward: quality, reliable sources, who accesses what.

Running a Big Data project (aborder un projet Big Data)

↑ contents
In plain words

A Big Data project is an IT project centred on data and its processing. It has two sides: the technology, and the business stake. The business side usually takes more effort: convincing decision-makers that data will improve processes, strategy and decisions. Because it differs from building a software product, think carefully about how to organise it, often with agile methods.

  • technological aspect (aspect technologique)Platforms, storage, processing, models.
  • business stake (aspect professionnel, enjeu métier)What value the data brings to the organisation, and convincing its leaders.
  • collaboration (collaboration)Business experts and data roles must work together from the start.
Worked example · putting the whole lesson together

A retailer wants to predict stock shortages from its sales data.

  • Project? Unique, new, sponsor = sales director, goal = "cut shortages by 20% within 9 months", budget set → yes (sections 1–2).
  • Real need (5 whys): shortages come from late orders, not from bad forecasts alone → include the ordering process (section 4).
  • Business stake: show the director the cost of a shortage before talking technology (section 14).
  • Team: Data Engineer + Data Scientist + Data Analyst from IT, store managers from sales → matrix (sections 6, 13).
  • Run it in 2-week Scrum sprints, each giving a usable forecast to the stores (section 12), and keep P, C, D in view (section 9).

  • Dates in the pre-project form. Mrs Jufer's credit request is dated 21.3.2020, but the pre-project it asks funding for starts on 15 March, 6 days earlier.
